Why Sherrelwood’s 29-foot median needs context

Among 5,826 records reporting depth, the median is 29 feet and the middle half runs from 20 to 40 feet. That describes a predominantly shallow dataset: 89% are under 100 feet. It does not establish the drilling depth for a new residential supply, especially with monitoring wells dominating local records.

Depth also has a long upper tail. The 90th percentile is 115 feet, while the deepest record reaches 1,751 feet. Permits identify Alluvial and Quaternary Alluvium wells, and the cost model assumes Denver Basin sedimentary bedrock. A useful bid must identify the intended aquifer and permitted use rather than price a household well from the overall median.

Reported yields are strong, but sparsely documented

Only 228 nearby records report pump yield. Their median is 20.0 gpm, with the middle half between 10.0 and 80.0 gpm. Recorded yields of 15 gpm or more make up 64%, but those figures combine well purposes and should not be treated as a household-well guarantee.

At the low end, 5% report less than 1 gpm. For a buyer, that means checking sustained output and recovery rather than relying on a pump turning on during a showing. A low-producing well may require storage and careful demand management. Reported yield also does not establish how much water a DWR permit legally allows you to withdraw or use.

Shallow groundwater does not show a falling trend

The 245 records with static water levels show a median depth to water of 10 feet, with the middle half from 6 to 20 feet below the surface. Static level is where water stands when the well is resting; it is not the total well depth or the water level while pumping.

Decade medians move from 30 feet in the 1960s to 10 feet in the 2010s and 8 feet in the 2020s. Those figures do not show a dropping water table. They also are not repeat measurements of the same wells, so they cannot prove recovery at a particular Sherrelwood property. Shallow groundwater warrants attention during excavation and septic feasibility work.

Sherrelwood’s permit activity is largely monitoring work

Permit activity increased from 402 records in the 1980s to 1,687 in the 1990s. The 2010s account for 2,536 permits, while the 2020s include 987; the newest permit year is 2025. The current decade is incomplete, so that comparison does not establish a slowdown. Of all nearby permits, 5,047 are recorded as constructed.

The leading driller-field entry is “Authorized (Pe, Pg)” with 3,353 records; treat that as a record label, not a verified drilling-company recommendation. Named firms include Layne, Christensen Co. (Tormoehlen, David A.) with 184 and Ground Exploration Llc (Bender, Joseph T.) with 157. Given the monitoring-heavy use mix, activity counts alone do not establish residential drilling experience.

What the Sherrelwood drilling estimate actually covers

The supplied cost model uses Denver Basin sedimentary bedrock at $50-$70 per foot. At the recorded median depth, it estimates about $1,400-$2,000 for the drilled hole and $7,400-$14,000 for a finished well with pump, tank, trenching and electrical.

That is a median-depth model, not a residential quote. The shallow monitoring-heavy records may not match the aquifer a household supply must reach. Ask a driller to state the target depth, casing and grout specifications, pump assumptions and access requirements. Have the estimate distinguish drilling from the complete working system, and explain how additional depth or an unsuccessful hole would be billed.

Environmental records deserve a parcel-level review

Within about 10 miles, the mapped inventory includes 699 PFAS sampling or contamination sites, 176 EPA Superfund or cleanup sites and 4 historic mine features. These are screening flags, not proof that a particular Sherrelwood well is contaminated. A sampling location does not necessarily represent a confirmed contamination finding.

Before buying, obtain available water-quality results and arrange testing appropriate to nearby site histories, including PFAS where warranted. Inspect the wellhead, casing and surface drainage. The 5% share reporting less than 1 gpm supports checking production carefully, but yield records do not measure dry-hole risk. Review septic separation, groundwater conditions and the proposed building footprint before committing to a layout.

Start with the permit before buying a Sherrelwood lot

First establish how the parcel is legally supplied with water. Do not assume a mapped well can serve a new home or that an existing household permit authorizes irrigation. Confirm the permit, approved uses and construction record with DWR, along with any applicable augmentation requirements.

Then compare genuinely similar wells around Twin Lakes, Welby, Shaw Heights and North Washington. Match aquifer and use before comparing depth or yield. For an existing supply, request a pumping evaluation, recovery observations and current laboratory results. For a build, coordinate drilling access, driveway placement, septic feasibility and utility trenching before fixing the house location. Make unresolved water-supply questions part of purchase due diligence.
