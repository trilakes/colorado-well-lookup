What to Expect From a Well in Teller County

Colorado Well Finder › Wells by county › Teller County

In Teller County, finding water means finding productive fractures in mountain bedrock. A nearby well is useful evidence, but it is not a guarantee that your lot will drill the same way.

Colorado DWR records include 10,770 permitted wells across the county, with 7,079 recorded as constructed. The reported median depth is 260 feet and median pump yield is 6.0 gpm. Those are useful starting points for a purchase or construction budget—not promises for a parcel in Florissant, Divide, Woodland Park or Victor.

Budget for Bedrock, Not Just the 260-Foot Median

Among the 7,315 wells reporting depth, the median is 260 feet, and the middle half run from 150 to 400 feet. For a driller’s bid, that spread matters more than the midpoint alone: ask how the price changes if drilling must continue beyond the planned depth. The 90th-percentile depth is 508 feet, so deeper work is part of the county’s record.

Most permits identify an unnamed or fractured-bedrock aquifer; others specify fractured granite or fractured crystalline rock. Denver Basin aquifers account for 0% of these wells. In this mountain setting, productive fractures—not a uniform underground layer—largely determine where drilling can stop. Compare nearby completion reports around Divide or Florissant rather than assuming countywide depth will fit your lot.

A 6.0 GPM Well Still Needs a Demand Check

The 4,334 reported pump yields have a 6.0 gpm median, with the middle half between 2.0 and 9.3 gpm. That range calls for matching the well’s tested performance to household demand, rather than choosing equipment from the county average. A reported pump yield is not a guarantee of sustained output through changing conditions.

The buyer’s concern is the low end: 11% report less than 1 gpm. A slow-producing well may need storage and careful demand management, and some results may not support the intended use. Before buying, have the well evaluated for sustained production and recovery. A neighbor’s strong well in Woodland Park or Cripple Creek does not establish what fractures your parcel will encounter.

Recent Water Levels Are Deeper, but the Record Is Limited

Static water level is the resting depth to water inside the well, not the depth of the drilled hole. Only 636 county records report it. Their median is 95 feet below ground, with the middle half from 60 to 140 feet.

Decade medians were 114 feet in the 1960s, 76 in the 1970s, 52 in the 1980s, 85 in the 2010s and 105 in the 2020s. The later measurements are deeper than those from the 1980s, suggesting lower resting levels in the more recent sample. However, these are not necessarily repeat measurements of the same wells. They do not establish a steady countywide decline. For a purchase, current static and pumping levels are more useful than treating this series as a forecast.

Teller County’s Permit Boom Was in the 1990s

The busiest permit decade was the 1990s, with 2,581 permits, compared with 1,799 in the 1980s and 1,791 in the 2000s. Records show 814 in the 2010s and 688 in the 2020s, with the newest permit year listed as 2025. The latest decade is incomplete, and permit totals are not counts of completed wells.

Leading historical driller entries are Mountain Well Improvement Llc (Wiley, David D.), with 1,774 records; Woods Drilling Inc. (Woods, William D.), with 1,604; and Woods Drilling Inc. What a New Teller County Well Can Cost

The supplied cost model uses $65–$100 per foot for drilling fractured mountain bedrock such as granite and gneiss. At the 260-foot median depth, that puts the drilled hole at about $16,900–$26,000. A finished well with pump, tank, trenching and electrical is modeled at $22,900–$38,000.

Treat those figures as planning allowances, not a parcel-specific quote. Get a written scope that spells out casing, grout, testing, access requirements and what happens if the planned depth produces too little water. On a mountain lot around Midland or Goldfield, rig access and the route between the well and house deserve attention before pricing is finalized. Confirm which permit and site-work expenses are included rather than assuming “finished well” covers everything.

Mine Features and Low Yields Deserve Separate Checks

Mapped environmental records within about 10 miles include 9 PFAS sampling or contamination sites, 276 historic mine features and 12 EPA Superfund or cleanup sites. These are screening flags, not proof that a particular well is contaminated. Around Victor and Cripple Creek, review mapped mine features alongside the parcel location and ask a qualified laboratory or local health authority which tests fit the site.

Water safety and water quantity are separate questions. Obtain laboratory results, inspect the wellhead and casing, and review construction and grout records. Fractured bedrock also carries low-yield and dry-hole risk; the county statistics cannot promise success. Locate the proposed septic system, replacement area, building footprint and well together before excavation so the layout meets applicable separation and access requirements.

Before Closing on a Lot in Divide or Florissant

Start with the actual DWR permit, not the listing’s statement that a property “has a well.” Match the permit and completion records to the parcel, confirm the well’s location, and check authorized uses. Domestic or household use appears on 91% of county records, but that does not mean every permit allows outdoor watering or livestock. Household-use restrictions and any augmentation requirements need a parcel-specific review.

Next, compare neighboring wells for depth and production, then obtain current testing for an existing well. For vacant land in Divide, Florissant or Woodland Park, have the driller and site contractor review access, the house location, septic feasibility and utility routes together. Resolve water supply and site-layout questions before committing to a building plan that depends on them.
